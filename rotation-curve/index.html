Galaxy Rotation Curves and Dark Matter

Build a galaxy from its visible parts and see whether it can explain how fast its stars orbit

Bulge mass Disc mass (stars and gas) Disc size (scale length) Dark matter halo strength Halo core size
The puzzle: almost all of a spiral galaxy's light comes from its central bulge and its disc of stars and gas, and that light fades away with distance from the centre. If that were all the mass, stars far out should orbit more slowly, just as Neptune moves more slowly than the Earth (the dashed "Kepler" curve). Instead, measurements of the speeds of stars and gas (using the Doppler shift of the 21 cm hydrogen line, for instance) show the rotation curve stays flat far beyond the visible galaxy. There must be extra, unseen mass spread out in a huge halo: dark matter. Vera Rubin's measurements in the 1970s made this case convincing.
Model: a Hernquist bulge, an exponential (Freeman) disc and a pseudo-isothermal dark matter halo, vhalo² = v∞²[1 − (rc/r) arctan(r/rc)], with speeds added in quadrature. The "measured" points are simulated from a typical galaxy of each type, with random errors, rather than data for a specific galaxy. In the animation, stars are sped up enormously; the Sun takes about 230 million years to orbit the Milky Way.
