The tug of war: gravity tries to pull a gas cloud together, while the pressure of the hot, moving gas pushes outward. The Jeans mass MJ is the smallest mass for which gravity wins. Hotter gas has more pressure, so it needs more mass to collapse (MJ ∝ T3/2). Denser gas has stronger gravity for its size, so less mass is enough (MJ ∝ n−1/2). That's why stars form in the coldest, densest places in a galaxy: molecular clouds at about 10 K. As a big cloud collapses its density rises, the Jeans mass drops, and it breaks up into many smaller clumps that each become stars. On the right, click anywhere to set the temperature and density.
Uses MJ = (5kT / GμmH)3/2 (3 / 4πρ)1/2 with mean particle mass μ = 2.33 (molecular gas), and the free-fall time tff = (3π / 32Gρ)1/2. Real clouds are also supported by turbulence and magnetic fields, which this ignores. The collapse animation is schematic, and the number of fragments is simply estimated as M / MJ. Preset values are typical, not for specific clouds.
