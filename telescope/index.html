Resolution: light passing through a circular opening spreads into a blob with faint rings (an Airy pattern), so even a perfect telescope blurs each star. Two stars can just be told apart when they're separated by the Rayleigh limit, θ = 1.22 λ/D. Bigger telescopes and shorter wavelengths give finer detail, which is why radio telescopes have to be enormous. On the ground, turbulence in the atmosphere (seeing) usually blurs stars to about 1″, whatever the telescope's size; that's a big reason to put telescopes in space or use adaptive optics. Light collection grows with the mirror's area, so it goes as D²: double the diameter and you collect four times the light.
The image is a simulation of two equally bright point sources through a perfect, unobstructed circular aperture. With the atmosphere switched on, the star image is approximated by a Gaussian whose width combines the diffraction limit and 1″ of seeing. Limiting magnitudes are rough visual estimates (6 for the eye, improving by 5 log₁₀(D / 7 mm)).
