Gravity slows time: general relativity predicts that a clock lower down in a gravitational field ticks more slowly than one higher up. Light climbing out of the field loses energy and is stretched to longer, redder wavelengths (gravitational redshift), which is the same effect seen from outside. At the Earth's surface the effect is tiny, about 0.06 seconds over a human lifetime, but it's real: atomic clocks show it even for a height change of 30 cm. GPS satellites must correct for it, or positions would drift by over 10 km a day. Near a neutron star or black hole the effect becomes enormous; at a black hole's event horizon, a distant observer would see a clock stop altogether.
Uses the Schwarzschild result for a non-rotating, spherical mass, dτ/dt = √(1 − rs/r) with rs = 2GM/c²; inside a planet or star the formula is different, so the clock stays at or above the surface. With "orbiting" switched on, the special-relativity slowing for a circular orbit's speed is added (exactly, dτ/dt = √(1 − 3rs/2r)). The animated clocks are sped up and, for small effects, the difference is far too small to see.
