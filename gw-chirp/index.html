Gravitational Wave Chirp

Two compact objects spiral together, sending out ripples in space that rise in pitch until they merge

Why it chirps: two orbiting masses continually stir up space-time, sending out gravitational waves that carry energy away. Losing energy, the pair spirals closer, orbits faster, and radiates even more strongly, so the wave's frequency and strength both climb: a "chirp". The gravitational wave frequency is twice the orbital frequency. Heavier pairs merge at lower frequencies, so black holes give a short, low chirp while neutron stars sweep up through the detectors' band for over a minute. The chirp mass, which sets how fast the frequency rises, is what the signal measures best. The first detection, GW150914, stretched and squeezed LIGO's 4 km arms by about a thousandth of the width of a proton.
The inspiral uses the leading-order (quadrupole) formula for a circular orbit, which is accurate early on but only approximate near the merger. The merger is placed where the pair reaches the innermost stable orbit, and the ringdown is an illustrative damped wave. Real signals are found by comparing data with detailed numerical-relativity waveforms. Strain is for an optimally oriented source.
