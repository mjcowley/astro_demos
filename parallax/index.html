The idea: as the Earth goes around the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift back and forth against much more distant background stars. The parallax angle p is half the total shift. A star with p = 1 arcsecond (1″ = 1/3600 of a degree) is at a distance of 1 parsec, which is where the word comes from: d (pc) = 1 / p (arcsec). One parsec is 3.26 light years, or about 206,000 AU. Even the nearest star has p < 1″, smaller than the blurring caused by Earth's atmosphere, which is why the first stellar parallax wasn't measured until 1838. Today the Gaia spacecraft measures parallaxes to about 0.00002″.
The left-hand diagram is not to scale: the nearest star is about 270,000 times further away than the Sun. The star is assumed to lie in the plane of Earth's orbit, so it shifts along a straight line; stars away from that plane trace out a small ellipse. The stars' own motion across the sky (proper motion) is ignored. Parallaxes from Gaia DR3 and Hipparcos, rounded.
