The Twin Paradox

One twin flies to a star and back at close to the speed of light. Who is older when they meet again?
Example:

Cruising speed Distance to the star
The paradox: each twin sees the other moving, so shouldn't each think the other's clock runs slow? The two situations aren't symmetric. The twin on Earth stays in one inertial frame the whole time. The traveller has to fire the engines to turn around, and can feel it; for the trip there and the trip back they're in two different inertial frames. Only the Earth twin can use the time dilation formula for the whole journey, so it's the traveller who comes back younger. The spacetime diagram shows it: the traveller's bent path between the same two events (leaving and returning) has less proper time than the straight one. Turn on the "now" lines to see the traveller's idea of "what time it is on Earth" jump at the turnaround.
The turnaround is treated as instant, and acceleration at the start and end is ignored; adding realistic acceleration changes the numbers a little but not the conclusion. Both twins are 20 when the trip starts. Distances are in light-years and times in years, so light travels at 1 light-year per year.
