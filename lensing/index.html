How it works: General relativity says mass curves space, so light passing a massive object is bent, just as a glass lens bends light. A galaxy or cluster sitting in front of a more distant galaxy can bend that galaxy's light around it. When the two line up perfectly, the light arrives from every side and forms an Einstein ring; slightly off-centre, it breaks into arcs or two or four separate images. The size of the ring depends on the lens's mass, so lensing lets astronomers weigh galaxies and clusters, including their dark matter. Lensing also magnifies the background galaxy, acting as a natural telescope.
Images are made by tracing light rays through a thin lens: a point mass, a singular isothermal sphere (a simple galaxy model) or an isothermal sphere plus external shear (an elongated lens). Distances assume a lens at redshift 0.5 and a background galaxy at redshift 2 in flat ΛCDM, with the Einstein radius computed for the point-mass case and used to scale the others. The background galaxy is an illustrative model.
