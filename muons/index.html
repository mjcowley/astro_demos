Muons, Time Dilation and Length Contraction

Short-lived particles from cosmic rays shouldn't reach the ground, yet they do. Relativity explains why, in two ways

The puzzle: cosmic rays hitting the upper atmosphere make muons, unstable particles that decay with a half-life of about 1.5 μs (mean lifetime 2.2 μs). Even at almost the speed of light, a muon could only travel about 660 m in 2.2 μs, so hardly any should survive the trip from 15 km up. Yet we detect lots of them at the ground. Earth's explanation: the muons are moving so fast that their internal clocks run slow (time dilation), so far fewer half-lives pass during the trip. The muon's explanation: from the muon's point of view it's at rest and the Earth rushes towards it, with the atmosphere squashed to a fraction of its thickness (length contraction), so the trip is short. Both give exactly the same number of survivors.
Each dot is a muon given a random lifetime drawn from the decay law with mean 2.197 μs. Counts at the ground are this random sample; the percentages in the readouts are the exact expected values. Real muons are produced over a range of heights and speeds, and some are absorbed; the Frisch & Smith (1963) experiment measured muons on Mount Washington (1907 m) and at sea level, selecting those with speeds near 0.995c.
