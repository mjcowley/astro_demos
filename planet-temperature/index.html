Planet Temperatures

How a planet's distance, reflectivity and atmosphere set its temperature

Energy balance: a planet warms up until it radiates away (as infrared) exactly as much energy as it absorbs from its star. Further away, the starlight is spread more thinly (inverse square law), so the planet is colder. A shinier planet (higher albedo) reflects more light and absorbs less. This gives the equilibrium temperature, which is what an airless planet would have. Greenhouse gases such as CO₂ and water vapour let sunlight in but trap some of the outgoing infrared, so the surface has to get warmer to get rid of the same energy. Equilibrium temperature assumes the absorbed energy is spread over the whole planet (a fast-rotating planet or one with winds); a slowly rotating airless world like Mercury has a much hotter day side and colder night side than this average. The greenhouse effect uses a simple grey-atmosphere model, Tsurface⁴ = Teq⁴(1 + ¾τ), with τ the infrared thickness of the atmosphere, tuned to match each planet. The habitable zone uses the conservative limits of Kopparapu et al. (2013), scaled with the star's luminosity. Gas giants also release internal heat, which isn't included.
