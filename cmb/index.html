The afterglow: for its first 380,000 years the Universe was so hot that atoms couldn't hold together; free electrons scattered light in every direction, like fog. When it cooled to about 3000 K, electrons joined protons to make neutral atoms (recombination), the fog cleared, and that light has travelled freely ever since. The expansion of space has stretched its wavelength by a factor of about 1100, so the glow that was once orange-hot now peaks in microwaves at just 2.7 K. It's almost perfectly smooth; its tiny ripples, a few parts in 100,000, are the seeds that grew into galaxies.
Temperatures follow T = 2.7255 K × (1 + z); ages assume flat ΛCDM (H₀ = 67.7, Ωm = 0.31) including radiation. The sky map is a simulation with the right statistical character but random spots, shown in galactic coordinates with a Mollweide projection and much less detail than real maps from WMAP and Planck. The dipole (3.36 mK, from our motion at 370 km/s) and the Milky Way band are simplified models.
