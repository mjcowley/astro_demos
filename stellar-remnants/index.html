Three ways a star can end: stars that start with less than about 8 times the Sun's mass leave a white dwarf, held up by electrons that refuse to be squeezed closer together (electron degeneracy pressure). Above about 1.4 M☉ (the Chandrasekhar limit) electrons can't do it, and the core collapses to a neutron star, held up by neutrons instead. Above roughly 2 to 3 M☉ nothing known can stop the collapse, and a black hole forms. Its size is the event horizon, the Schwarzschild radius Rs = 2GM/c² (about 3 km per solar mass). Notice that a heavier white dwarf is smaller: more mass squeezes the electrons harder.
White-dwarf radii use the Nauenberg (1972) mass-radius relation; neutron stars are taken to be about 12 km across all masses, and the maximum neutron-star mass (about 2.2 M☉) is uncertain. A teaspoon is 5 cm³ at the object's average density. A black hole has no surface, so a "teaspoon" and average density aren't really meaningful for it. Sirius B is the white dwarf companion of Sirius.
